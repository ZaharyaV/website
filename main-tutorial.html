This is tutorial 2 (part 1) of my neurodivergence-friendly tutorial series for Scrivener 3. It’s intended for people who are new to Scrivener, as well as people who are already familiar with it, as it covers the major features in quite a bit of detail.

Important note: All my tutorials are based on Scrivener for MacOS! Though minor, there are some differences between Scrivener 3 for Windows vs for MacOS, so you may notice a few small discrepancies if you use Scrivener with Windows.
Further, all tutorials are based on my own personal workflow, and all screenshots are from my personal files, so please don’t distribute those elsewhere. My workflow is not universal (duh), and if you use certain features differently that doesn’t mean one of us is doing it wrong. You do you!
Lastly, this tutorial originated on Discord, which is probably noticeable in how it’s written – I am not changing that, since many people have insisted that this was exactly what made it easier to follow than the official Scrivener tutorial.

Main Tutorial (Part 1)

Alright, so you’ve just installed Scrivener, or you know Scrivener but want to learn more about it – so we’ll pretend you just installed it for a second. Yes? Yes.

As could be expected, you have to create a new project first of all – and seeing that you’re here, you’ve already skipped over the Getting Started options that pop up first. So you’re probably looking at this:

Personally I recommend to pick a suitable Project Template for your needs. Are you writing Fiction? Non-fiction? Scripts? The choice of project template matters because each project template comes with a particular preset of root-folder structure, section types, and compile formats that suit the needs of whichever type of document you’re writing, as well as potentially certain types of prepared Document Templates (e.g. Character/Place sheets for Novels vs Style Guidelines for Research Proposals). You can adjust all that in the settings, but that’s not part of this tutorial here. [Further information about all these things can be found in the continuing tutorials.]

Making a bold assumption here, most people reading this are probably fiction writers; so we’ll go with Novel with Parts here. Am I biased because NwP is my favourite? Maybe.

“But V, my story doesn’t have Parts!”
Yeah okay, I mean– alright, fine; if you are writing a story that very definitely does not have Parts, just ignore my yammering and choose Novel.

Once you have picked a project template, it will ask you to save the project-file somewhere on your computer; give it a file-name and save it wherever you save your writing documents. Scrivener will then open the brand new project, and you’ll see something like this:

Let’s have a look at the main elements of the interface: Spanning the top you have the Toolbar, which we’ll get into in detail towards the end of this tutorial. Then, on the left you have the Binder, in the middle there’s the Format Bar with the Editor(s) right below, and on the right you have the Inspector open on the first tab. [Note that this arrangement is simply the default layout and there are myriad options to customise the interface (beyond what I mention here)! ]

For example, if you’re anything like me and have a visceral need to have all the things visible at once, you might have a big enough screen for something like this:

[Yes, now the personal document screenshots started – again pls don’t distribute those and also don’t judge my years old, raw, unedited writing please okthxbye.]

So, this is what I usually look at when I write: On the left of the Split view (which I love) I got the view of the whole thing I’m currently writing, so I can scroll back and forth there, while I usually work on the right. Again, I have a big external screen which makes the Split view super comfortable – despite the fact that my ADHD demands at least 150% zoom otherwise my brain cannot compute. But when I’m on my laptop screen there’s only really space for one file, or I split the Editor horizontally rather than vertically.

To get into Split Mode, click the little Split-icon in the top-right corner of your Editor; to switch between horizontal and vertical Split Modes, Option-click the same icon (you’ll see it change as you press Option).

Regardless of how much I love split view though, for the following screenshots it’ll all be in the small screen version, since not everyone has a big external screen.

Well then, let’s get into it!


The Binder

The Binder on the left is where your folders and text files are organised. As already mentioned, I use Novel with Parts (NwP), so the basic preset structure inside the Manuscript folder matches that: First-level folders are Parts, second-level folders are Chapters, and the text files inside are Scenes. [From now on, if I say “file”, I am referring to these text files, not the .scriv Project file that sits on your desktop somewhere! ] You can also stack several files.

The NwP project template presets exemplary folders named Part and Chapter, but you can name your folders and files whatever you want. If you leave a folder or file unnamed, the Binder will show the first few words of either the text itself or, if there is one, the Synopsis – don’t worry we’ll get to that in a bit.
Here’s a closer look at an example from one of my long fics:

Generally, the idea is to do your actual writing in files (Scenes), but it is also possible to write directly in a folder. To start with, I’d recommend keeping your text in files though, since most preset export formats are set up with that assumption and text that sits directly in a folder might not show up on compile – but more on that later (much, much later).

So what exactly is inside a file? I generally write one-scene-per-file in my manuscript; having multiple scenes in the same file only works with short one-shots with tiny scenes for me. But that’s just me – you can totally have several scenes in the same file if you want to – whatever works best for you in your specific project. 

Outside of the Manuscript, your files can contain anything you need them to – in my case it’s mostly notes. This includes Character and Place sheets, which have their own root-folders each in NwP projects; if you create a new file inside one of these root-folders, it will automatically match the preset Character and Place sketches. That’s because these preset Character sketches and Place sketches are Document Templates that are included in any of the Fiction-type Project Templates (Short Story, Novel, Novel with Parts).

[Other Project Templates may include other preset Document Templates, but in turn will not include these.]

Status and Labels

Both folders and files can have a Status, as well as a Label. You can set them in their respective drop-down menus in the bottom-right corner of the interface, or via right-click  on the folder(s)/file(s) in the Binder itself.

The Status generally refers to, well, the status of the thing – Default options include In progress, First draft, Revised draft etc. Default options for Labels are 6 basic colours.

Both Label and Status options are customisable! Simply click Edit to rename, add, edit or remove options.

“But why would I do that?” – So glad you asked!

For example, in the screenshots above you’ll see that the icons of all the folders and files have different colours in my Binder; that’s because I adjusted my settings so that folders and files show the Label colour. In my case, the Label is (primarily) tied to the POV; that way I can see at a glance how frequent any given POV is, and how they’re roughly distributed. For fics that I started posting before they were complete, I also turned a few key Statuses into Labels, to easily see what’s posted. But that’s totally individual and up to you if and how you’d wanna use labels. I just like it colourful.

Besides that, you can Filter by Status and Label in the Project Search, and make (automated) Collections based on your search criteria. But that’s a topic for the Extended Tutorial. Anyway, where were we? Right.

What you select in the Binder is what will be shown in the Editor; for example, if you select the whole Manuscript root-folder, all folders and files contained within the Manuscript will be shown in the Editor. Alternatively, you could select a part folder, a chapter folder, or a single scene — depends on what you wanna work on etc. If you select a folder (on any level) or multiple files, all files within the folder or selection will be shown in a continuous scroll, separated by dashed lines. This is called a Scrivening. The word count at the bottom of the Editor indicates the total word count of the selected Scrivening (or file if you only selected one).

You can also import external files by simply drag-and-dropping them into the Binder. For example, you could import a PDF of a paper into the Research folder so you have it easily accessible while you work.


The Editor

The Editor in the middle has three main view modes: Scrivenings mode (shown in the screenshot above), Corkboard mode, and Outline mode. To switch, click the respective symbol right of the Quick Search bar in the middle.

So far, all the screenshots showed the Editor(s) in Scrivening mode – the mode that displays the content/text of whatever you have selected in the Binder. [Images of the other two modes are coming up further down.]

Above the Editor(s) is the Format Bar, which is pretty much the same as most format bars; you can change your font, font size, colour, highlight colour, alignment – and so on and so forth. Note that the appearance of your text in the Editor is not necessarily the same as the output when you compile the project! [Further details in the Compile tutorial.]

There are a couple other options for the Editor (including Typewriter mode, Focus, and Composition mode); they are covered in the Extended Tutorial.

Copyholder

Now I’ve already talked about layout and the Split modes further up, but that’s not the end of our layout options in the Editor. Besides Split mode to show two files/scrivenings, you can also use the Copyholder to display notes, a snapshot (we’ll get to that), another scene – whatever. To do that, you right-click on the file you want and go to Open -> in Copyholder. You can change the position of the Copyholder with a right-click into its frame.

Similarly, if you choose Open -> as Quick Reference, the selected file will open in a small, floating window. Many options to have many things open at once – neat shit.

[There are a couple other options for the Editor (including Typewriter mode, Focus, and Composition mode); they are covered in the Extended Tutorial.]


The Inspector

So, finally, all to the right we have my favourite thing: the Inspector, with five tabs. [Reminder at this point that this is just my personal workflow. Ok? Ok.]

Notes

The first tab (outer left icon) is Notes. With Notes selected, up top you see Synopsis. I use this to roughly outline my scene. Sometimes there’s no more than a sentence or two in there, in this case now it’s a fairly detailed one.

Under that you see the Notes, and you see that not everything is in the same font. I put many things in the notes, the three main ones of which you see in the first image above:

  • something that’ll potentially go into the author’s notes when I eventually post the chapter with that scene in it; aka my thoughts while writing, how i felt, something funny, idk — really whatever, just author’s notes
  • shit I wanna remember without having to think about it every time; here, who’s in the scene and the age of the three most important characters
  • in the serif font you see deleted sentences! meaning sometimes while writing I’m like “nah, i wanna say this differently” but I also am not sure how, so instead of deleting the thing outright I cut-paste it into the side notes and keep it. Sometimes it gets used later, sometimes it won’t.

Every now and then I’ll clear out the side notes, for which (in some projects) I made a dedicated Deleted Passages root-folder to collect it all in. Whenever the side notes get too full, I cut-paste the whole blurb into the deleted passages file for that scene. Though, admittedly, it’s quite rare that I keep multiple and organised files of my deleted passages. Usually it’s just one huge thing where I never find anything.

Still, I’ve legit brought back whole long sections of text from this deleted passages log. Like, sometimes I’d bring back up to 700-1000 words that barely needed edit, it just had to go somewhere else and I wasn’t there yet. And I still do that, even though I by now discovered the Snapshot function. We’ll get to that in a bit.

Bookmarks

Moving on to the next Inspector tab, we’re not with Notes anymore but with Bookmarks! So, wtf are Bookmarks, how do they work, why the fuck do we care?

In essence, a Bookmark allows you to open linked files in the Inspector. That’s neat because your Editor can stay where you were and you can continue writing, rather than clicking away from your scene there to check your notes — which then means you have to find your spot again once you return. Nobody likes that. 

(Yes, you could right-click on your desired internal file to open it in the Copyholder. That’s effort though, plus now you can’t use the Copyholder for something else. Anyway, moving on.)

There are two “types” of bookmarks: Project Bookmarks (PB) and Document Bookmarks (DB) – yes i’m abbreviating those because I’m lazy. You can easily switch between the two like this.

And because Scrivener is amazing, both of these do exactly what it says on the tin.

Project bookmarks are accessible from everywhere and anywhere in the project. No matter which folder, file, or scrivening you’re in, PBs are always there for you, like the kind of reliable person that is too mentally stable to be friends with any of us and brings us tea when we’re cold.

“Okay, great, but why do we need that?”

Well, no matter what you’re writing (thesis, novel etc.) there are probably certain things you’re gonna look up more than once (if you’re anything like me and your memory is as sound as sieve at least). General notes, outlines etc. seem like the obvious choices to be linked as PBs — you’ll likely need them over and over, and a PB lets you access the thing(s) from anywhere quickly. Neat shit.

Document bookmarks on the other hand are specific to the file where you created the bookmark (shocking, right? I’d never have guessed that). So if you bookmark a thing for the third scene in chapter 14, the DB won’t show up anywhere else. 

“But V, you just said that we can make things accessible from everywhere, why would we not just do that?”

Right, sure, fair point. Until there’s this side character which is kinda prominent in those two scenes but not really anywhere else and you keep forgetting whether you gave them armour or not, or whether they’re tall or tiny, and where did you say they came from again? Are you sure you want to clog up your project-wide bookmarks with the half-empty character sheet for Obscure-Sidekick #36? Exactly.

Now, though I’m sure it’s already quite obvious from above: What exactly do you actually save as a bookmark?

Internal Bookmarks (IB) are any files from within your Scrivener project that you select over the Binder-menu thingy you see in the image. Those are the notes, the outlines, the character and location sheets, the research you’ve imported — whatever, you could even link another scene that is really closely related to the one you’re working on with a DB.

External Bookmarks (EB) are the logical counterpart of IBs, meaning anything that isn’t in your Scrivener project. Is there this one website you always inevitably open while writing? Bookmark it.

EBs also include External File Bookmarks which allows you to link a file from your computer to keep it on hand; like the PDF of that one Review article that covers the entire theory for your thesis and you’re tired of going back and forth while trying to paraphrase.

Now those of you who paid attention saw a thing named Matching Text. The way this works is that it’ll compare the text of the doc you’re currently in with the text in all the bookmarks and show you any matching text. I’ll admit I have not yet found a good use for this, except if you’re writing a research paper and wanna check that your paraphrasing holds up. I’m sure there’s a point to it and it’ll reveal itself eventually – until then we’ll gloss over this here.

Metadata

Moving on from Bookmarks, we go one further to the right to the little tag icon: There we have General Metadata, Custom Metadata, and Keywords.

General Metadata is literally just that; ordinary metadata. Boring, moving on.

The only preset that I saw for Custom Metadata is “Event Start Date” – I added Event End Date myself by clicking on the three little dots which opens the settings. You can pick your date format (short, long, full, including time, custom etc.) and there are three other types of metadata (Text, Checkbox, List) that I haven’t explored yet. 

I assume if you have something that can either be true or untrue for a scene a checkbox would make sense.

Keywords are really quite simple: To set it up, you either use the menu bar (Project) or click on the three little dots in the Keywords field and choose Show Project Keywords. Now in the beginning that’s obviously empty – so you fill it with … well, with whatever you want! 

I sorted mine by character and location, but you do what you need for your project. The left little plus is to add a keyword on the same level (sibling), the one to the right of it creates a sub-keyword (child), and the minus to the right of that deletes whatever is selected.

You can shift the keywords around as much as you like, can drag and drop them into each other to make them child-keywords etc. Though personally I’d create it as linearly as possible because then it makes a pretty rainbow by default! Whiii!! Of course you can also change the colours so… ANYWAYS

To add a keyword to a file (folder, scene, whatever), you either open the Project Keywords and drag-and-drop (works with multiple at once!) or you click on the three little dots again and choose your tag you wanna add.

Now why is this cool? It’s cool because a) if you go into the Project Keywords, select one and then click the little magnifying glass (bottom right), it’ll show you all the files/folders that have that Keyword in the Binder (neat shit), and b) COLOURS!

Corkboard view, for example, looks like this:

And Outline view looks like this:

You can also display the actual text of the tags, I chose to have them as colour chips. You can pick and choose what is displayed in the outline; word count, progress, synopsis etc. Further, Outline view also gives you the option to display any Custom Metadata you made.

Snapshots

Moving on from Keywords, we go on to Snapshots, which has a little camera icon. Snapshots can be either Titled or Untitled. If the file you’re snapshotting has a Status set, untitled Snapshots will automatically include the Status in parentheses.

“Okay but what even is a Snapshot?”

Basically, a Snapshot is exactly what the name implies: a snapshot of your scene at a given time, like a version-safe! First of all it helps to avoid losing anything by accident, lovely. But, more importantly, it allows you to preserve the state of a scene before editing. That’s particularly nice in cases where you want/need to make significant changes but might not have fully settled on the specifics of it all. Take a Snapshot, then edit – you could even make multiple edits and take a Snapshot of each if the story keeps refusing you (gods know I’ve been there).

You can then compare your current file with your Snapshots, which – again – is neat as fuck if you ask me. If you want you can adjust to your desired level of specificity for the comparison, then click Compare. The differences between the selected Snapshot and the current file contents will be highlighted in red (deleted) and blue (added new).

You can view Snapshots like above in the Inspector (which can be broader, by the way; you could make it half your screen if you wanted), or you can view it in the Copyholder (with or without changes) like this:

Frankly, Snapshots were a whole revelation to me when I first discovered them, and I adore them.

Comments & Footnotes

Now, it should be noted that I, personally, do not really use Comments and Footnotes. Somehow that just hasn’t found its way into my workflow. The only time I ever used them was to easily find certain paragraphs in a research note. They are fairly simple though and work pretty much the same as everywhere else. Click the speech-bubble icon to add a Comment, click the cf icon to add a Footnote. Comments and Footnotes are highlighted in the text and listed in the Inspector like this:

Summary

Let’s wrap this up then, shall we? In summary, those are the things we covered:

  • Creating a new project; basic structure of the Novel with Parts template
  • Interface; default layout overview
  • Binder – levels, labels, status, selecting one or multiple folders/files (= Scrivenings), importing files via drag-and-drop
  • Editor – main view modes (Scrivening, Corkboard, Outline), format bar, Copyholder
  • Inspector – notes, bookmarks, metadata and keywords, snapshots, comments & footnotes

Since this whole tutorial originated from me telling Tacky about my workflow specifically, I’ll include the list of my personal favourites as well – for anyone who came here to get inspiration on how to use features they already knew.

So, these are the features that I, personally, find most valuable and how I use them:

  • Binder with Label-colour = POV and completion status
  • Split-view Editor so I can scroll to past scenes on the left, write on the right (or just one open file if I’m on my laptop)
  • Synopsis to sketch my scene; anything between 1 sentence and detailed outline
  • Notes for author’s notes, shit I wanna remember (date, character ages, sunset time — whatever is relevant to the scene), and cut-pasted deleted sentences
  • Bookmarks to link notes, character sheets etc.
  • Keywords for characters, locations, relationships or whatever else I wanna tag
  • Snapshots to preserve versions of my scenes before I edit or if I change my mind about something (or just to double save it)
  • Note files for outlines, future passages/scenes, research, general notes etc.
  • Character and Place sheets
  • Deleted Passages to clear out the side notes every now and then and keep my deleted passages in case I do go back and decide I want them anyways
  • Outline view if I need to take a look at it from a distance
  • Corkboard view to get a different kind of overview

Overall it’s very ADHD friendly which is great for me; all that chaotic organisation speaks to me.

But the secret to any of these functions really is: You use everything however the fuck you want. You can ignore half of it, if it’s just not for you – I do too. Only use what works for you and use it how it works for you.

And there you have it — Scrivener! (Well, mostly.)
